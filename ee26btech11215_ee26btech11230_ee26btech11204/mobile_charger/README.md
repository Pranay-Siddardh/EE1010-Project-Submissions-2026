# Project Journey

This project began as an attempt to build a charger with an automated enclosure and a mechanism for opening and closing the cable compartment. We initially explored a servo-based mechanical design and developed the first enclosure around the available components, using the dimensions of a small servo as a reference. As the mechanical design became more complicated, with hinges, pulleys, grooves, moving parts, and multiple compartments, we realised that the construction and assembly would take much longer than the available project time. We therefore abandoned the first enclosure and returned to the electrical side of the project. The second stage focused on building a reliable 5 V charger using the transformer, bridge rectifier, filter capacitor, and 7805 regulator described in the initial project documentation. After getting the basic circuit working, we experimented with additional features such as touch-based control, an LED indicator, an ESP-01S, and eventually an ESP32.

The project then evolved into a dual-output charger with separate 5 V and 12 V buck converters and a relay for selecting the output. We developed the ESP32 control logic and initially designed an OLED-based interface, but the physical OLED did not work even though the simulation worked correctly. We replaced the display with an ESP32 webserver, which allowed the output to be selected from a phone. Even after the webserver was working, we faced further physical debugging problems, including incorrect connections on the buck converter and, during the final third attempt, the relay not reliably responding to the ESP32's 3.3 V GPIO level. For the final prototype, we used an AAA battery to add approximately 1.6 V to the 3.3 V control level, giving approximately 4.9 V at the relay input and allowing the relay to operate reliably. After these successive changes, failures, redesigns, and tests, we completed the final dual-output charger and integrated the circuit into the final 3D enclosure. The project therefore became a practical journey through circuit design, embedded programming, debugging, mechanical design, and iterative engineering rather than simply a direct implementation of the original idea.
